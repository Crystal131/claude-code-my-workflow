## 1. Big Idea: Choose the Right Tool for the Job

- Research methods work like the tools in a toolbox. Each one suits some jobs and not others.
- A good researcher knows the options and **matches the method to the research question**.

---

## 2. The Control (Constraint) Continuum

Research methods differ in **how much control (constraint)** the researcher has over the thing being studied.

```
LESS CONTROL  ◄──────────────────────────────────────────►  MORE CONTROL
  Archival methods        Descriptive methods          (Experimental —
  (secondary data)        (primary data)                later modules)
```

| Low control | High control |
|---|---|
| More open and flexible (often **qualitative**) | More structured and precise (often **quantitative**) |
| You **pay a price**, such as less precision | You **pay a price**, such as less flexibility and naturalness |

**Key point:** No level of control is best everywhere. How much control you use depends on:
- What the situation allows
- How much control you *need*
- What trade-off ("price") you are willing to accept

## 3. Archival Methods (Lowest Control)

### Definition
An **archive** is a collection of documents. The **archival method** covers any research method that uses **only secondary data**.

| Primary data | Secondary data |
|---|---|
| Collected **by the researcher** | Collected **by someone else for another purpose** |

- **Historical research** uses only the archival method, and it is a type of **qualitative** research.

### Why every study needs archival work: the literature review
- All research has to start from **what is already known**, so you don't reinvent the wheel.
- The **literature review** (written into the **Introduction**) should set the stage for the methodology.
- ✅ If it is written well, the reader can **anticipate your methodology** by the end of the Introduction.
- 📌 **Course action item:** Once you pick a topic, start searching the **library databases** right away. The Introduction is a **required group assignment**.

### Advantages and disadvantages of secondary data

| ➕ Advantage | ➖ Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| **Easy to access.** You just look it up. | **Quality/validity?** Do the data really measure what they claim to? Was the study done properly? |
| | **Fit?** The data may not match your needs (e.g., you need it broken down by grade level, but it isn't). |
| | **No raw data.** Usually you only see the analyses and descriptive statistics (mean, median, mode, SD), so you **can't disaggregate** or re-analyze. |

- 🥇 **Gold standard:** **Refereed (peer-reviewed) journal articles.** Publication in a quality journal gives high confidence in the findings.

### What archival methods do
- They **describe what is already known**, so in a sense they deal with the past.
- They can still offer **fresh insights or reinterpretations** by combining studies in new ways.
  - Boyer calls this **the scholarship of integration**.

### Types of archival research

| Qualitative | Quantitative |
|---|---|
| Historical research | Review articles of quantitative studies |
| Case studies | **Meta-analysis** |
| Narrative literature reviews | |

### Meta-analysis ⭐
- A **statistical procedure** that **combines data from many independent studies** (different journals, different authors) to find an **overall effect size**.
- It works by combining the descriptive statistics each study reports.
- **Benefits compared with narrative reviews:**
  - A more **objective** appraisal of past research
  - A more **precise estimate** of a treatment effect
- Narrative reviews are still valuable and can offer fresh insights.

**Example: meta-analysis on spanking and children** *(published about 4 years before the lecture; this appears to be Gershoff & Grogan-Kaylor, 2016)*
- **75** previously published studies, **160,927** children in total, far more than any single study could include
- All **survey** research. Experiments on this topic would be neither **ethical** nor **practical**.
- Found **13 detrimental outcomes**, including:
  - More aggression and antisocial behavior
  - Mental health problems
  - Negative relationships with parents
  - Lower moral internalization (understanding society's dos and don'ts)
  - Lower cognitive ability
  - Lower self-esteem
- ⚠️ **Caution:** These results are **correlational, not causal**. It is hard to separate the effects of spanking from other parenting and environmental variables.

## 4. Descriptive Methods (Middle of the Continuum)

- **Purpose:** To **describe** phenomena in new ways using **new, primary data**.
- They sit in the **middle** of the control continuum.
- Quantitative descriptive methods are often called **correlational methods**.

### 4a. Correlation

**Definition:** A statistical technique that measures the **degree of relationship** between two or more **quantitative variables**.

- The most common version is the **Pearson product-moment correlation**, symbolized **r**.
- **Range:** −1.0 to +1.0

| Feature of *r* | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| **Sign (+ / −)** | **Direction** of the relationship |
| **Absolute value** (ignoring the sign) | **Strength** of the relationship |

- **Positive (direct):** high values go with high values.
- **Negative (inverse):** high values on one variable go with **low** values on the other.
  - *Example:* The higher the dose of an effective medication, the lower the mortality rate, so dose and mortality have a **negative** correlation.
- **r = ±1.0** is a **perfect** correlation. It is rare, so most results are decimal fractions.

### 4b. ⚠️ Correlation ≠ Causation

Two things can go together without one causing the other. A **third variable** may cause both.

**Examples from lecture:**
| Correlated events | Real explanation |
|---|---|
| Japanese (saucer) magnolia blooms → neighbors get the flu | Both happen in **spring** |
| Per-capita cheese consumption ↔ deaths from tangling in bedsheets | Spurious / coincidence |
| Marriage rate in Maine ↔ drownings after falling from fishing boats | Spurious / coincidence |

- **Extraneous variables** (also called **exogenous variables**) are variables *outside* the relationship that may explain it.

**How we infer causation in everyday life:**
1. **Covariation.** The two things go together (cause present → effect; cause absent → no effect). *Necessary but not sufficient.*
2. **Temporal precedence.** The **cause must come before the effect**.
3. **Close proximity** in time.

> 🍣 *Sushi example:* If you eat sushi and get sick that night, the sushi **may** be the cause. If you were sick *before* eating sushi, the sushi **can't** be the cause, because the effect can't come before the cause.

### 4c. Cross-Sectional vs. Longitudinal Designs

🥖 **Metaphor: a loaf of French bread**
- **Cross-sectional** means slicing across the loaf like sandwich bread: **one slice, one point in time**.
- **Longitudinal** means slicing lengthwise like a po'boy or hoagie: **following along over time**.

| | Cross-sectional | Longitudinal |
|---|---|---|
| **When data are collected** | One point in time | Several points in time |
| **Who** | Usually several **different groups**, compared with each other | The **same group**, followed over time |
| **Also called** | — | **Repeated-measures** research |
| **Example** | Political poll comparing Democrats vs. Republicans, men vs. women, education levels | Giving the same questionnaire to the same people today, in a month, and a month after that |

## 5. Common Types of Descriptive Methods

### 5a. Naturalistic Observation
- **Observing behavior in its natural setting.**
- It is common in anthropology, for example studying great apes (orangutans, gorillas, chimpanzees) in the wild instead of in an artificial zoo setting.
- It can use **qualitative, quantitative, or both** kinds of variables.

**Examples from lecture:**
1. **Grocery shopping efficiency by gender** (consumer behavior class)
   - *Dependent variable:* **shopping efficiency**, defined as **number of items purchased per unit of time** (timed from entering the store until the first item was placed on the checkout counter).
   - *Result:* **No statistically significant difference** between men and women.
2. **Territoriality in a university library.** How people react when someone takes "their" seat (social psychology).
3. **Mothers' vs. fathers' speech to young children** (instructor's dissertation; the children were toddlers learning language, around 18 *months*).
   - *Result:* **No differences.** Mothers and fathers were equally sensitive to their children's language needs.

#### Problem: Reactivity
- **Reactivity** is the degree to which people **change their behavior because they know they are being observed**.
  - *Example:* A researcher in a white lab coat with a clipboard follows a shopper, and the shopper skips buying an embarrassing personal hygiene item.

#### Solution: Unobtrusive Observation
- **Observing people without their awareness** ("being sneaky").
  - *Example:* The instructor watched a child in the classroom during ADHD evaluations **before** the child knew who he was.
- **Is it ethical?** ✅ **Yes**, as long as it takes place in **public** spaces. Researchers have the same right as anyone else to observe people in public.
  - ❌ Following people into **private** places is **unethical and illegal**.

### 5b. Interviews
- The **most common qualitative method**. It is associated with the qualitative approach called **phenomenology** (covered later).
- Usually uses a **structured interview protocol**, meaning a set list of questions.
- It still needs **flexibility**, so the interviewer can **follow up** on interesting answers ("Tell me more about that…").
- **Analysis:** Look for **emerging themes**, which are similar ideas expressed by different interviewees, even in different words.
- The themes are **categorized and named**, so they become **qualitative variables**.

### 5c. Survey Research ⭐ (most common method in the behavioral sciences)

| ➕ Advantage | ➖ Limitation |
|---|---|
| **Very efficient** for collecting descriptive data from **large numbers** of **respondents** ("quick and dirty") | **Always relies on self-report.** All we know is what people tell us. |
| Sometimes the only way to get the information, and people are the best source | **Are people telling the truth?** Sampling and measurement issues also arise. |

- Surveys **cannot establish causation**. They show what people **think** affects what, not what actually does.

#### 📌 Terminology: say it precisely!
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Survey** | The **method** |
| **Questionnaire** (or **survey instrument**) | The **data collection instrument** |

> ❌ "I'm going to distribute surveys."
> ✅ "I'm going to use the **survey method** to distribute **questionnaires**."

- Survey research is usually **cross-sectional**. It compares groups such as gender or political affiliation.

#### 📌 Course project connection
- You will use **crosstabs (cross-tabulations)**, a simple descriptive tool, to look for patterns in your data.
- **Target: about 100 questionnaires.** With only 40–50, patterns are hard to see.

## 6. Key Terms Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Control / constraint continuum** | How much control a method has over the phenomena studied, from low (archival) to high |
| **Primary data** | Data collected by the researcher |
| **Secondary data** | Data collected by someone else for another purpose |
| **Archival method** | Any method that uses only secondary data |
| **Historical research** | Qualitative research that uses only archival (secondary) sources |
| **Literature review** | A synthesis of prior research that sets the stage for the methodology |
| **Scholarship of integration** | (Boyer) Creating new insight by combining existing studies |
| **Meta-analysis** | A statistical combination of many independent studies to get an overall effect size |
| **Effect size** | The size of a relationship or treatment effect |
| **Descriptive methods** | Methods that describe phenomena using new primary data |
| **Correlation** | A statistical measure of the degree of relationship between quantitative variables |
| **Pearson r** | Correlation coefficient from −1.0 to +1.0; sign = direction, absolute value = strength |
| **Inverse (negative) relationship** | High values on one variable go with low values on the other |
| **Extraneous (exogenous) variable** | An outside variable that may explain an observed relationship |
| **Covariation** | Two variables vary together. Needed for causation but not enough to prove it. |
| **Cross-sectional** | Data collected at one point in time, usually comparing groups |
| **Longitudinal / repeated measures** | Data collected from the same people at several points in time |
| **Naturalistic observation** | Observing behavior in its natural setting |
| **Dependent variable** | The outcome being measured (e.g., shopping efficiency) |
| **Reactivity** | Changing behavior because you know you're being observed |
| **Unobtrusive observation** | Observing without the person's awareness (ethical in public settings) |
| **Structured interview protocol** | A set list of interview questions, with room for follow-ups |
| **Emerging themes** | Recurring ideas across interviewees that become qualitative variables |
| **Survey** | A research *method* |
| **Questionnaire / survey instrument** | The data collection *instrument* in survey research |
| **Self-report** | Data based only on what respondents say about themselves |
| **Crosstabs** | A descriptive table showing patterns across categories of variables |

## 8. Big-Picture Summary

| Method | Data | Control | Qual/Quant | Main strength | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Archival** (historical, lit review, meta-analysis) | Secondary | Lowest | Both | Easy access; builds on what's known | Quality and fit of data; no raw data |
| **Naturalistic observation** | Primary | Middle | Both | Real behavior in a real setting | Reactivity |
| **Interview** | Primary | Middle | Qualitative | Depth; follow-up possible | Labor-intensive; interpretive |
| **Survey** | Primary | Middle | Mostly quantitative | Efficient with large samples | Self-report; no causation |

**Take-home message:** Descriptive and archival methods can show *what is* and *what goes together*, but **they cannot establish cause and effect**. Methods with more control, covered in the next modules, are needed for that.
